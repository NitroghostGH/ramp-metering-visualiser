Ramp metering, in one minute

A freeway carries the most traffic when its density sits right at a critical value. Let too many vehicles in and it tips past that point: speeds collapse, and — counter-intuitively — the number of vehicles that actually get through drops. That is a capacity drop, and once a bottleneck breaks down the jam spills back for kilometres.

Ramp metering puts a traffic signal on each on-ramp and releases vehicles at a controlled rate, keeping the merge just below the tipping point. This tool compares three strategies on real Queensland corridors: no control, local ALINEA, and coordinated HERO — optionally with variable speed limits.

Open this while the simulation runs — switch scenarios and watch the road, the queues and the space–time map respond.

The fundamental diagram

Flow (veh/h) versus density (veh/km/lane). Up to the critical density the road is in free-flow and flow rises with density. Past it the road is congested: adding vehicles only lowers speed and flow. Metering aims to hold the operating point near the peak — the critical occupancy target ô.

Occupancy (what loop detectors measure) is proportional to density, so “target occupancy” and “critical density” are the same idea in different units.

ALINEA — local feedback metering

rj(k) = rj(k−1) + KR · ( ô − oj(k) )

ALINEA (Asservissement LINéaire d'Entrée Autoroutière, Papageorgiou et al. 1991) is a simple integral feedback controller. Every control period each on-ramp measures the occupancy oj just downstream of its merge and nudges its metering rate rj up or down to drive that occupancy toward the target ô.

  • KR — regulator gain (larger = faster but jumpier). Try the slider.
  • ô — target occupancy at capacity (~critical density).
  • rmin, rmax — the rate is clamped so the ramp never fully stops or floods.

It is local, model-free, provably stable and has been field-proven for decades — which is exactly why it is the base layer of most managed motorways.

HERO — coordinated metering

Local ALINEA is greedy. The ramp nearest a bottleneck fills its short storage first; once it is about to spill back onto the surface street it is forced to release — and the bottleneck breaks down anyway, while upstream ramps still had empty storage they never used.

HERO (Papamichail & Papageorgiou, Monash Freeway 2008) keeps every ramp on ALINEA but adds a light-touch coordination layer. When an active bottleneck ramp starts to fill, it becomes a master and recruits upstream ramps to hold vehicles back too, balancing how full each ramp's queue is. Storage is shared across the whole group, so no single ramp overflows and the bottleneck stays protected for longer.

Switch between Local ALINEA and HERO in the app and watch the queues: under HERO they spread upstream instead of piling onto the ramp nearest the bottleneck. HERO approaches the performance of full optimal control while needing only real-time measurements.

Variable speed limits (VSL)

Ramp metering controls the on-ramps; VSL controls the mainline. By lowering the posted speed limit upstream of a bottleneck (Mainstream Traffic Flow Control), the motorway itself is gently metered — smoothing the flow into the merge and buying the ramp controllers more room. In the app VSL is a second feedback loop you can layer on top of any strategy.

In Queensland

TMR / Transmax's STREAMS Smart Motorways run exactly these ALINEA / HERO algorithms. HERO coordinated metering was evaluated on the M1 / M3 (Faulkner, Dekker, Gyles, Papamichail & Papageorgiou, 2014); ramp signals operate on the Pacific, Gateway and Logan motorways and the Bruce Highway.

The corridors here are representative models: real route, interchange, lane and speed-limit data with approximated chainages, demands and geography.

Using your own traffic data

The Demand data panel lets you replace the built-in AM-peak with a real demand profile. Upload a CSV like this (values in veh/h):

time_min, mainline, Klumpp Rd, Marshall Rd, Kessels Rd
0,   2600,  600,  700,  750
5,   2900,  700,  820,  880
10,  3200,  820,  950, 1010
…

time_min is minutes from the start; mainline is the upstream entry demand; each remaining column is an on-ramp, matched to the corridor by name. Use Template to download a correctly-headed file pre-filled for the selected corridor, edit it with your counts, and upload. The demand-level slider still scales everything, so you can stress-test a real day.

This is the first step toward full real-data support — loop-detector demand drives the model directly. Observed occupancy/speed overlays for validation are a natural next addition.
