Lesson 01 · The problem

Why meter a ramp?

A lane of freeway carries the most vehicles when its density sits right at a critical value — around one car every 30 m at speed. Let too many in and it tips past that point: speeds collapse and, counter-intuitively, the number of cars that actually get through falls. Traffic engineers call that lost throughput the capacity drop — typically 10–15%. Once a bottleneck breaks down, the jam spills back upstream for kilometres.

Ramp metering puts a signal on each on-ramp and releases vehicles at a controlled rate, holding the merge just below the tipping point. The trade is deliberate: a short, managed queue on the ramp buys a freely-flowing mainline.

No controlramp runs free → merge breaks down → jam spreads
Meteredramp holds back → merge stays fluid → throughput kept
Keep this open while the simulation runs behind it. Each lesson ends with a Try it — a control to move so you can watch the idea play out.

Lesson 02 · The core relationship

The fundamental diagram

Everything rests on one curve: how flow depends on density. Below the critical density the road is in free-flow and flow climbs as more cars join. Above it the road is congested and flow falls. The model uses an exponential speed–density law:

1 V(ρ) = vf · exp[ −1a (ρρcr)a ] speed falls smoothly from free-flow as density rises

Flow is just speed × density × lanes, which gives the humped curve below. Loop detectors don't measure density directly — they report occupancy, the % of time a vehicle sits over the sensor, which is proportional to density:

2q = ρ · V(ρ) · λ
3o = 100 · ρρjam

The solid curve is the bottleneck's own relation q = ρ·min(V(ρ), limit): its posted speed limit gives the straight low-density branch, with the unconstrained V(ρ) shown faint behind. The gold point is capacity — peak flow, at critical density. The glowing dot (with its fading trail) is the bottleneck's live measured state as the sim plays, exactly like a detector's flow–density scatter: watch it climb toward the peak and, without control, slip into the congested branch — falling below the curve. That gap is the capacity drop, and the loop the trail traces is breakdown hysteresis. With VSL restricting, an amber dashed curve shows the controlled zone's reshaped diagram under the posted limit — lower peak, critical density pushed right. That reduced peak is the cap on what the zone can feed the bottleneck, which is exactly the mainstream-flow-control mechanism. Switch scenarios to compare.

Worked example · where is capacity? With vf = 110, ρcr = 33.5 and a = 1.867, the speed at critical density is 110 · exp(−1/1.867) ≈ 110 × 0.585 ≈ 64 km/h, so one lane's capacity is q ≈ 33.5 × 64 ≈ 2 150 veh/h. The target occupancy is o = 100 × 33.5 / 180 ≈ 18.6% — that is the number the controllers aim for.

Lesson 03 · The simulation

Modelling the flow — METANET

The corridor is sliced into ~½ km segments, each holding a density ρi and a speed vi, updated every T = 10 s. Two equations govern each segment. First, cars are conserved — what flows in minus what flows out changes the density:

4 ρi(k+1) = ρi(k) + TL λi [ qi−1 − qi + ri − si ] density change = (inflow − outflow + on-ramp − off-ramp) over the segment

Second, speed relaxes toward the fundamental-diagram value while reacting to what's ahead. Each coloured term does one job:

5 vi(k+1) = vi(k) + Tτ[V(ρi) − vi] + T viL[vi−1 − vi] − νTτL · ρi+1 − ρiρi + κ
  • Relaxation — pulls speed toward the equilibrium V(ρ) over time τ.
  • Convection — carries the speed of the segment upstream into this one.
  • Anticipation — drivers slow for higher density ahead.

Two more terms (not shown) add the local slow-down where an on-ramp merges and where a lane drops. When a bottleneck is pushed past critical, its discharge is capped below capacity — that is the capacity drop of Lesson 01, and the reason metering pays off.

Lesson 04 · Local control

The integral form means small errors keep nudging the rate until occupancy sits exactly on target. It is model-free, provably stable, and field-proven — which is why it's the base layer of most managed motorways.

Lesson 05 · Coordinated control

HERO

Local ALINEA is greedy: the ramp nearest the bottleneck fills its short storage first and, once it's about to spill onto the surface street, is forced to release — so the bottleneck breaks down anyway, while upstream ramps still had empty storage. HERO (Papamichail & Papageorgiou, Monash Freeway 2008) keeps every ramp on ALINEA but shares the storage. Each ramp tracks a fill ratio:

7 fj = wjwjmax how full ramp j's queue is, from 0 (empty) to 1 (about to spill)
  1. Every ramp computes its own ALINEA rate (Eq. 6).
  2. A ramp whose occupancy is at target and whose fill ratio exceeds the recruit threshold becomes the master.
  3. The master recruits the nearest upstream ramp, tightening its rate so its fill ratio rises to match — spreading the queue upstream.
  4. Any ramp truly at its limit is released, so nothing spills onto the arterial.

Worked example · sharing the queue The bottleneck ramp is nearly full (f = 0.9) and at target occupancy → it's the master. The upstream ramp has room (f = 0.3). Because 0.9 > 0.4 (the recruit threshold), HERO cuts the upstream rate so its queue grows toward the master's fill. Net effect: the bottleneck ramp stops overflowing, the mainline stays protected, and total delay is shared instead of dumped on one ramp.
Try it Flip between Local ALINEA and HERO and watch the ramp queues: under HERO they spread upstream instead of piling onto the ramp nearest the bottleneck. Lower the HERO recruit threshold to make it share earlier.

Lesson 06 · Mainline control

Variable speed limits

Ramp metering controls the on-ramps; VSL controls the mainline. Lowering the posted limit upstream of a bottleneck (Mainstream Traffic Flow Control) meters the motorway itself — thinning the flow arriving at the merge and buying the ramp controllers headroom. The model runs the two functions Queensland gantries run. Flow control: a limit proportional to how far the worst detector runs over target occupancy is posted over the VSL zone length upstream of the bottleneck, capping speed in the fundamental diagram (Eq. 1). Queue protection: once detectors read a queue (a run of segments crawling below ~45 km/h), speed-matched signs cover the whole queue and follow its back as it grows — extending upstream immediately, retracting only on the hold clock — so drivers never run into an unprotected queue tail. Signs post in 10 km/h steps, change at most 20 km/h per sign hold time, and an approach taper caps every drop at 20 km/h per segment (…80 → 60 → 40, never a cliff). Protection alone costs a little mainline speed — the throughput win appears when VSL is layered on ALINEA or HERO with the Enable VSL switch.

Lesson 07 · The real world

In practice

These are not textbook toys. TMR / Transmax's STREAMS Smart Motorways run exactly these ALINEA / HERO algorithms; HERO coordinated metering was evaluated on Queensland's M1 / M3 (Faulkner, Dekker, Gyles, Papamichail & Papageorgiou, 2014) after its debut on Melbourne's Monash Freeway. Ramp signals operate on the Pacific, Gateway and Logan motorways and the Bruce Highway.
